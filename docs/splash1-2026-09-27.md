### Shipped first-pass composition

[knot.ts](../src/app/scene/knot.ts) now loads the optimized mining rig alongside deterministic instanced rocks, stars, inexpensive procedural planet bands, and three directional lights plus hemisphere fill. No shadows, bloom, audio, or machinery animation are enabled. The model's authored orange surfaces remain painted material detail; no emissive map was fabricated.

For desktop and iPad visual exploration, drag or swipe to orbit around the rig; scroll or pinch to zoom within bounded distances. Panning is disabled. Manual input pauses automatic drift, which resumes three seconds after the interaction ends **from the selected camera angle**, without restoring the old view. Reduced-motion preference disables automatic drift but leaves manual orbit available. Orbit controls are active only while the mining scene is ready and visible; they and the canvas touch-action override are released on scene exit. The existing locale-driven on-screen hint explains the controls.

The instanced debris shares a spherical exclusion zone for terran planets, gas giants, and stars. Only rocks that would intersect this zone are moved radially outward from the body's center. Placement includes each rock's deformed geometry and largest scale axis, with a 0.25 scene-unit gap beyond the radius-4 body and its 1.012-scale terran cloud shell. Counts and seeded variation remain unchanged. Rocks may still overlap the body's silhouette in projection when they are in front of it; this is 3D surface clearance, not a screen-space exclusion rule. Gas-giant rings and translucent stellar effects are not part of the spherical exclusion zone.

The rendered celestial body is additionally placed four times farther from the default splash camera and scaled by four, preserving its perspective silhouette while putting its entire spherical surface behind the asteroid field in the normal view. This applies to terran planets and clouds, gas giants and their rings, and stars and their effects. Placement is recalculated for responsive framing and retained when a body is rebuilt through the controls; it does not follow manual orbit. The rig, debris transforms, zoom limits, and orbit freedom remain unchanged, so orbiting around to the opposite side can still reverse the foreground/background ordering.

The wavy procedural planet shader was replaced with an Earth-albedo map on the same sphere, sharing the image URL with the existing [Earth component](../src/app/component/earth.ts). The splash requests only the albedo JPEG, not the bump map. The original JPEG is 8192 x 4096 (about 170.7 MiB as RGBA8 with mipmaps), so the splash downsizes its own copy to 2048 x 1024 for standard or 1024 x 512 for low quality _before GPU upload_. The source image still downloads at about 4.4 MB and may occupy significant transient CPU decode memory; the resized GPU texture is estimated at about 10.7 MiB standard or 2.7 MiB low. This experiment makes a third-party request to the `nartc/threejs-earth` GitHub repository (the user's chosen short-term option); if that request or image preparation fails, the splash reports an error and retains the poster and retry control. The upstream README describes the imagery as NASA/ESA-sourced with potentially required attribution; resolve exact image provenance/attribution and consider self-hosted approved imagery before shipping publicly. Recheck load time and mobile memory.

Local browser verification observed the remote image rendering on the planet without console warnings or errors. WebGL texture upload inspection confirmed a 2048 x 1024 canvas for standard and 1024 x 512 for a mobile-width low tier. Those visits used a browser cache (the image request returned 304), so their displayed readiness times are not cold-transfer or iPad measurements.

The DOM overlay shows a locally rendered poster and glTF loading progress while the scene prepares. Progress is the existing Angular Three/Soba loading-manager progress (assets/items), not a byte-accurate transfer meter; the UI explains that first-frame preparation can take longer. Errors are logged and shown with retry, and users can choose the still image during or after loading. No-WebGL2 environments retain the poster and controls. GLB and poster requests carry source-hash version tokens.

For temporary iPad profiling, a small badge in the upper-right corner of the scene identifies the selected standard (154k) or low (50k) GLB and reports seconds from the start of its 3D load attempt until the scene is ready. It says "3D loading" before readiness and "Still image" for the fallback. The duration includes model retrieval, decoding, and first-frame preparation, **not** initial application/page startup; it can benefit from browser cache. The badge is intended to be removed after device testing.

The camera and background are restored when the scene leaves. Geometry, textures, materials, instanced buffers, and loader workers are owned by the scene and disposed; late loader results are discarded. The shared canvas caps splash DPR at 1.5 standard / 1 low, uses demand mode for still/hidden views, and retains the other scenes' rendering policy.

### Validation evidence and limitations

- Both uncompressed source remeshes passed glTF validation without errors or warnings.
- Both compressed exports report zero validator errors, but the bundled validator does not support `EXT_meshopt_compression` or `KHR_texture_basisu`. It warns about KTX2 MIME/image recognition; these warnings are documented, not silently treated as complete validation.
- All four extracted KTX2 payloads passed `ktx validate`. Real browser loads successfully decoded both optimized models with the configured Meshopt/Basis loaders.
- All 60 focused Vitest tests passed, covering intro transition, route/shell regressions, tier selection, deterministic composition, and resource disposal. The later orbit-controls pass added Playwright mouse and touch interaction checks (including drift resumption and reduced-motion behavior); all seven splash tests passed. The previously run leave-game regressions also passed. The splash spec is registered in the 3D test partition and its npm commands.
- Production Angular build passes, with the existing unrelated cold-boot-scan CSS budget warning. Build output includes the optimized assets/poster/decoder but excludes the supplied source exports, which are no longer in the repository, and the separately excluded direction image. Development serving does not expose the source GLBs as public assets.
- Ten repeated static/3D transitions held steady at four geometries and three renderer textures after each reload. This verifies stable GPU resource counts, not a full heap/listener leak audit.
- Reduced motion retained the exact same camera position and renderer frame counter across a one-second observation, in demand mode.
- Standard runtime: four draw calls, 164,533 triangles, 750 star points. Low runtime: four draw calls, 56,015 triangles, 750 star points.

**Hardware interpretation matters:** the user reports a 20-core Core i9, 32 GB RAM, and two graphics cards with a 5090 primary. The interactive browser actually reported `NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080` through ANGLE/D3D11; these figures are not attributed to the 5090. At a 1442 x 902 browser viewport, DPR 1.25 and the default split, a 30-second interactive sample collected 1,801 frames: median 16.7 ms, p95 16.9 ms, maximum 18.3 ms. This is a high-end desktop check, **not** evidence of minimum-spec or real-mobile performance.

The automated headless browser instead used **SwiftShader software rendering**. Its 1440 x 900, DPR 1 standard scene measured median 133.3 ms and p95 150.1 ms over 30 seconds. This fails the animation frame budget on that software backend; it must not be confused with native-GPU performance or a real mobile device.

Cold-cache development-server measurement at 10 Mbps / 100 ms RTT: standard asset started at about 7.44 seconds after navigation and completed transfer in 6.49 seconds; the complete scene was ready at about 16.10 seconds (8.66 seconds after the model request). The poster completed at about 7.62 seconds. The post-request scene budget passed, but the 2.5-second initial-poster target did **not** pass in this development build. Production cold-start/LCP, native lower-end GPU/mobile frame budgets, byte-level GPU memory, decoder-failure/context-restoration stress testing, and a full route-cycle heap audit remain outstanding. The 390 x 844 mobile-viewport check validates low-tier selection and rendering only.

The present result is a functional first implementation for visual feedback, not a declaration that every proposed release gate below has passed.

## 3. Why the original must not be the production splash asset

At a sustained 10 Mbps, the original alone requires approximately **141 seconds** to transfer; at 50 Mbps, approximately **28 seconds**. These are idealized transfer calculations before latency, contention, decoding, shader compilation, and GPU upload.

Geometry alone represents about 139.79 MiB of GPU buffer payload. Assuming RGBA8 texture allocation and full mip chains, the three textures would consume approximately **192 MiB**. Together that is roughly **332 MiB** before framebuffers, shadows, other scene objects, decoder overhead, or CPU-side copies. Actual allocation is renderer/device dependent and must be measured.

Reducing only texture size would leave roughly 140 MiB of geometry. Geometry compression reduces transfer size but does not, by itself, reduce triangle count or the decoded geometry workload.

There is also a delivery issue:

- [angular.json](../angular.json) copies everything under `public` into build assets. Merely avoiding a runtime reference does not remove the source from deployment.
- Angular's initial JavaScript bundle budget is not a budget for an independently fetched GLB.
- The original exceeds GitHub's ordinary 100 MiB per-file limit. Do not commit it as a regular Git blob.

Keep the original unchanged while planning. Before implementation ships, agree on source storage outside deployed assets, or a precise asset-copy exclusion. If versioning the original is desired, agree on Git LFS or other approved storage and its hosting implications first. Never upload it to an external optimizer without approval.

## 4. Recommended visual design

### First implementation

Use a curated, noninteractive composition rather than a free-orbit model viewer:

1. **Hero:** optimized mining rig, viewed at an angle that exposes its most recognizable industrial surfaces.
2. **Planet:** textured low-segment sphere behind the hero with an inexpensive atmosphere/rim treatment. Avoid a physically simulated atmosphere in the first pass.
3. **Asteroid field:** a small collection of shared low-detail rock geometries, instanced with deterministic positions, scales, and rotations.
4. **Distant depth:** star points and inexpensive distant debris; do not represent every distant speck as a high-detail mesh.
5. **Lighting:** restrained environment/fill lighting and one directional key. Add authored emissive accents to sell orange vents and white work lights.
6. **Motion:** very slow camera drift or limited parallax, with an entirely still reduced-motion mode. No audio or input capture by default.

Use the reference's palette and depth without promising an exact real-time reproduction. Dust, animated cranes, moving ships, volumetric beams, and cinematic camera sequences are later enhancements, contingent on assets and frame budget.

### Fit the actual application layout

The reference is landscape, but the application's default split gives the scene roughly half the screen. Compose against the **right-panel dimensions**, not the full browser width. Keep the recognizable hero silhouette and planet rim legible at the default split, narrow mobile widths, and user-resized panels.

Do not change the split layout, authentication forms, or intro timing implicitly. Remove the old in-scene `/knot` presentation label when replacing the visual, while keeping route compatibility.

## 5. Asset preparation pipeline

1. **Establish a visual baseline.** Open the source locally in Blender or a compatible glTF viewer; record its best angle, material appearance, and silhouette. Confirm the usable front orientation and whether back faces are actually required.
2. **Create derivative copies.** Keep the supplied files intact. Start from the new 154k and 50k Meshy remeshes rather than repeating simplification. Further local simplification in Blender is contingent on visual/performance results. Preserve UV seams, normals, thin structural members, and the hero silhouette.
3. **Bake where needed.** If remeshing changes topology/UVs, bake source detail onto the new mesh. Do not assume simple decimation preserves texture quality. Inspect cranes, railings, cavities, and panels at the intended screen size.
4. **Prepare two quality tiers.** The supplied remeshes already meet the 150,000-250,000 standard and 40,000-80,000 low-quality hero triangle targets. Reduce their maps to at most 2K for standard and 1K for low quality. These are starting targets, not guarantees of acceptable appearance.
5. **Correct materials.** Preserve glTF color-space semantics: base color/emissive use sRGB; normal/metallic/roughness remain linear data. Review the JPEG normal map for artifacts. Author selective emissive detail rather than making the entire base-color map glow.
6. **Optimize delivery.** Evaluate local glTF Transform tooling for pruning, deduplication, geometry quantization/compression, and KTX2/Basis texture conversion. Prefer one geometry codec, selected after comparing size, decode cost, and visual results; do not stack alternatives unnecessarily.
7. **Wire decoder support before enabling compressed assets.** The existing `GLTFLoader` usage does not establish Draco, Meshopt, or KTX2 support. Configure the selected decoder/transcoder, self-host its runtime files, verify deployment paths/CSP, and include their bytes and startup cost in budgets.
8. **Produce a poster.** Render the approved composition into a responsive, compressed image. Use this immediately while 3D loads and when 3D is unavailable. The direction image is not automatically the final poster.
9. **Validate and record.** Run a glTF validator, check dimensions/counts/size, compare renders with the original, and record the source fingerprint, tool versions, exact processing settings, and output hashes.

Suggested derivative names: `asteroid-mining-rig.standard.glb`, `asteroid-mining-rig.low.glb`, and `mining-splash-poster.webp`. Version or hash runtime asset URLs explicitly; do not assume copied public assets receive bundle filename hashing.

Do not fetch standard and low variants simultaneously or load both just to choose one. Start with a conservative selected tier. Add upgrades only if profiling justifies the additional transfer and transient memory.

### Implementation structure and lifecycle

- Implement a standalone Angular Three splash scene using the existing shared canvas and signal/computed conventions. Keep composition and asset loading local until actual reuse warrants extraction.
- Add a small typed splash state only where needed to bridge the scene and its DOM poster/status overlay: idle, loading, ready, error, and intentionally static.
- Keep the poster/status layer outside the Three.js scene graph. Keep login, registration, and navigation usable independently of asset readiness.
- Load only optimized local assets when the splash is relevant. Initially preserve the five-second intro transition; show the poster during subsequent loading. Evaluate idle prefetch only after measuring contention with the initial UI.
- Transition off the poster only after the selected model, textures, and required environment resources can render a complete frame, not merely after the HTTP request finishes.
- On asset/decoder errors, log through the existing `appLogger`, retain the poster, show a localized unobtrusive status, and provide an explicit retry or static-view action. Do not silently claim the 3D scene loaded.
- Handle unsupported WebGL/context loss at the canvas host so the fallback works even when the scene component cannot initialize.
- Respect reduced motion, hidden documents, and actual scene occlusion. Demand mode is useful only if splash animation callbacks are not continually invalidating hidden/static frames.
- Cancel owned requests where supported and guard late async completion after navigation. Release event listeners, animation hooks, geometry, textures, materials, and owned image resources. If assets are cached, define ownership so unmounting one consumer cannot dispose another consumer's resources.
- Restore camera, background, environment, exposure, and renderer settings changed by the splash. Avoid global renderer-quality changes that affect game scenes.
- Add new user-visible strings to both [en.ts](../src/app/i18n/locales/en.ts) and [it.ts](../src/app/i18n/locales/it.ts).

No backend or socket-contract changes are needed for a decorative splash.

| Estimated splash-owned GPU resources                                                     | At most 128 MiB                                      | At most 64 MiB                                  |
| Device pixel ratio cap                                                                   | 1.5 initially                                        | 1 initially                                     |
| Real-time shadows                                                                        | Off initially; benchmark one limited light if needed | Off                                             |
| Postprocessing                                                                           | None initially; bounded bloom only after profiling   | None                                            |
| Sustained motion                                                                         | Target 60 fps; p95 frame interval at most 20 ms      | Target 30 fps; p95 frame interval at most 36 ms |
| Static/reduced-motion state                                                              | No continuous splash animation invalidations         | Poster or demand-rendered still                 |

Frame-time and transfer budgets must be met together. A tiny compressed download can still be expensive to decode/render. Low-tier selection should use supported capability/preference signals conservatively, not user-agent strings alone; preserve a static fallback and avoid quality-tier oscillation.

### Measurement protocol

- Record hardware, OS, browser, graphics backend, viewport, panel split, DPR, quality tier, asset hashes, and cold/warm cache state.
- Test desktop at 1440 x 900 with the default split and a representative mobile device at 390 x 844. Include narrow/wide panel resizing.
- Use a repeatable cold-cache network profile of 10 Mbps download and 100 ms RTT. Record CPU throttling separately; a desktop browser under throttling does not replace real mobile testing.
- Target usable public controls and poster within 2.5 seconds of navigation under the chosen profile. Record full-page LCP separately from poster timing; preserve or improve baseline if existing application startup is slower.
- Under that network profile, target the complete standard scene within 12 seconds of the asset-load trigger, low tier within 6 seconds. The existing five-second intro delay is additional and must be reported separately.
- After a five-second warmup, capture at least 30 seconds of animation and report p50/p95 frame intervals, long frames, draw calls, triangles, and effects/shadow passes.
- Use browser network/performance traces for bytes, decoding, upload stalls, and responsiveness. Use `renderer.info` for counts, not as a GPU byte meter; estimate resource bytes from formats/dimensions and corroborate where tooling permits.
- Repeat enter/leave cycles ten times. Verify owned resource counts and listeners return to baseline, or to a documented bounded cache plateau, with no monotonic memory growth.
- Verify the original 176.52 MB source is neither requested nor present in the production artifact.

## 8. Delivery phases and exit criteria

### Phase A: approve scope and asset baseline

- Confirm scene placement, motion, target devices, and storage for the original.
- Render and review the source locally; inventory the other supplied models.
- Capture the current navigation and startup performance baseline.
- Exit: approved framing direction and a feasible source-to-web asset workflow.

### Phase B: optimize and approve the hero

- Produce standard/low derivatives and a provisional poster.
- Validate files and record reproducible processing settings.
- Compare thin structures and surface detail at real panel sizes.
- Exit: both asset variants satisfy transfer/triangle budgets and visual review.

### Phase C: integrate a minimal scene

- Replace the knot visual behind the compatible route.
- Add hero, basic lighting, camera, poster, explicit loading/error handling, and reduced-motion support.
- Preserve current intro/authentication/character flows and resource cleanup.
- Exit: usable controls during loading, reliable re-entry, and no production reference to the original.

### Phase D: compose and profile

- Add planet, instanced asteroids, stars, and authored emissive details.
- Add restrained motion; evaluate bloom only after baseline measurements.
- Tune quality, responsive framing, and resource budgets.
- Exit: approved screenshots and measured performance on target hardware.

### Phase E: regression and release

- Run focused tests and the Angular production build.
- Verify deployment asset exclusion, decoder paths, caching, and error cases.
- Record final metrics and any approved deviations.
- Exit: all acceptance checks below pass, with a rollback to the previous scene implementation available through version control.

## 9. Validation plan

Follow [testing-policy.md](testing-policy.md); use Vitest, not Jest.

### Focused Vitest coverage

- Quality selection and supported-capability/reduced-motion decisions.
- Loading, ready, explicit failure, retry, and late completion after teardown.
- Camera/resource ownership and restoration.
- Existing intro timer cancellation, primary-versus-left outlet behavior, route mapping, and character-setup navigation.

Start with [intro.vitest.ts](../src/app/page/public/intro.vitest.ts), [routed.routes.vitest.ts](../src/app/routed.routes.vitest.ts), and new focused splash tests. Include shell/character tests when those surfaces change.

### Playwright coverage

- Initial arrival and direct `/knot` navigation; preserve left-panel interactions while assets load.
- Login/registration before the intro timer expires, with deterministic socket mocks.
- Delayed asset response, missing/corrupt model, decoder failure, and static fallback with usable controls.
- Reduced motion, panel resizing, narrow viewport, and scene overlay visibility.
- Leaving gameplay, logout, and return to character list without canvas injection errors; extend [leave-game-session.spec.ts](../e2e/tests/leave-game-session.spec.ts) where appropriate.
- Repeated route entry/exit and no late callbacks updating a destroyed scene.
- At least one real optimized-asset rendering smoke test; mocked loader success is not evidence that the actual GLB renders correctly.

Use explicit readiness assertions and deterministic asteroid placement. Use Playwright Clock `fastForward()` for intro timers rather than processing every animation frame. Keep hardware performance profiling separate from correctness assertions on variable-speed CI workers.

Run focused impacted tests first. After template integration, run `npm run build` to validate Angular templates; TypeScript-only checking is insufficient. Also inspect the build output for asset sizes/exclusions because ordinary bundle budgets do not enforce the model budgets.
